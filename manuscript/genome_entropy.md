# Title

Structural entropy distinguishes protein-encoding genes from spurious open reading frames.

# Authors

<!-- Author list, affiliations and contributions are built from Author_Information.xlsx (see README.md). -->

# Abstract

Most open reading frames (ORFs) in a microbial genome do not encode proteins, yet gene callers choose among them from nucleotide statistics alone, without asking whether the translated sequence resembles a protein. In this study, we asked whether the information content of predicted protein structural tokens separates real genes from spurious ORFs. With genome_entropy we computed the Shannon entropy of each ORF's amino-acid sequence and of its Foldseek 3Di structural string, which a protein language model predicts from sequence alone. We applied it to 2.62 billion ORFs from 199,837 GTDB species representatives, 90 billion six-frame ORFs from 50,009 assembled metagenomes, 6.75 million ORFs of at least 90 aa and 102 million of 10–90 aa from 36,025 phage genomes, and 43.7 million curated small ORFs.

Annotated genes and unannotated ORFs separated most prominently on 3Di entropy rather than amino-acid entropy. In bacteria, the mean difference was 2.08 bits for 3Di against 0.39 bits for amino acids, and 87.6% of unannotated bacterial ORFs had 3Di entropy below log₂3, the most that a string of three structural states can reach. The structural signal persisted at fixed ORF length, was specific to the translated frame, ranking a gene's own frame first among its six frames in 92.4% of genes, and carried most of the information about which phage ORFs are annotated. Compared with shadow ORFs matched on length, entropy and truncation, about 51,000–59,000 ORFs in GTDB genomes behaved like protein-coding genes missing from GenBank annotation. A strict individual test retained 25,114 candidates, giving a conservative floor of about 5,400 genes, while an independent gene caller suggested an upper bound of about 200,000. In metagenomes, 41.9% of high-scoring intergenic ORFs that Prodigal did not call had full-length structural homologues, against at most 0.45% of matched controls, and in phages the approach recovered a replication gene lost during re-annotation. Below about 100 amino acids, the structural entropy signal weakened, suggesting 100 amino acids is an approximate lower length limit for the method.

# Introduction

Almost every analysis in microbial genomics, from metabolic reconstruction to comparative and evolutionary studies, starts from a list of protein-coding genes. Gene callers such as Prodigal [@Hyatt2010-qv], GeneMarkS [@Besemer2001-un] and Glimmer [@Delcher2007-uf] build that list from nucleotide statistics: codon usage, coding potential, start codons and ribosome-binding sites. Generally, these methods are accurate for complete bacterial genomes, but are less so where those statistics are weakest. These include genomes with short genes, fragmented contigs, unusual codon usage, and on metagenomes in which a contig may come from an organism unlike any the model was trained on.

However, none of these statistics asks directly whether the translated sequence looks like a protein. Most ORFs in a genome are not genes. In every six-frame translation, the ORFs of real genes are outnumbered by their own shadows in other frames on the same strand and on the opposite strand, and by intergenic ORFs. 

We can use information theory to compare predicted ORFs and protein-encoding ORFs. We previously used Shannon entropy to describe the composition of genomes and metagenomes [@Akhter2013-pl]. We reasoned that real proteins are constrained by folding and function, and so this constraint should show less in amino-acid composition than in structure.

Protein language models now make structure fast and cheap to approximate. Foldseek's 3Di alphabet describes each residue's local tertiary interactions in 20 states [@Van_Kempen2024-il]. Models such as ProstT5 [@Heinzinger2024-ja] and ModernProst [@modernprost] predict 3Di strings directly from amino-acid sequences, fast enough to encode every ORF of a genome, or of a metagenome.

Here we test whether the entropy of predicted structure separates protein-coding genes from spurious ORFs, and whether it finds genes that annotations missed. We compare structural evidence with matched controls using genome_entropy [@Edwards2026-jk] at four scales:
1. **GTDB r232:** the species representatives [@Parks2022-eo], to describe the entropy landscape of annotated and unannotated ORFs across bacteria and archaea, and to estimate how many genes GenBank annotation misses.
2. **LOGAN:** 50,009 assembled metagenomes [@Chikhi2025-ak], comprising 90 billion six-frame ORFs, to test the signal beyond ORF length, across reading frames and biomes, and against Prodigal's own calls.
3. **Inphared:** 36,025 phage genomes [@Cook2021-ay], a gene-dense case in which we rank candidate missing genes and examine an annotation error.
4. **GMSC small ORFs** [@Duan2024-ab], to find where the method stops working.



# Methods

## Overview of the genome_entropy framework

All entropy features were computed with genome_entropy (https://github.com/linsalrob/genome_entropy) [@Edwards2026-jk]. The pipeline takes nucleotide sequences (from either a GenBank or FASTA format file) and:
1. calls every open reading frame (ORF) in all six frames;
2. translates each ORF with the bacterial genetic code (NCBI table 11);
3. predicts a structural-alphabet representation of each translated ORF;
4. calculates per-ORF entropy features.

When an input record carries CDS annotations, each ORF is also labelled by whether it matches one of them. Different datasets were processed with different package releases, encoding settings and hardware. The configuration for each is given in Table 1, and its effect on the values is quantified under *Reproducibility of the structural encoding*.

## ORF calling

ORFs are maximal stop-to-stop open reading frames reported by `get_orfs` [@Edwards2024-cn], so an ORF extends upstream of any start codon to the preceding in-frame stop. Each ORF records whether it is complete or runs off a contig end.

The minimum-length argument behaves differently by source of data. The GTDB, phage and mutual-information datasets have an effective shortest sequence of 90 amino acids. The LOGAN analysis explicitly set the shortest sequence to 30 amino acids. The GMSC small ORFs are a curated set of ORFs from 10–99 amino-acids and were not ORF-called. <!-- src: methods_provenance.md §1; Validation/gtdb/scientific_report.md l.135–141; #103 --> The phage genomes were also re-encoded with ORFs of 10–90 amino acids (Methods, *Short phage ORFs*).

## Annotation labels

For datasets with deposited annotations (GTDB, phage), an ORF was labelled as matching a CDS when all of the following held:
- it lies on the same record and strand as the CDS;
- its biological start is in compatible codon phase with the CDS's, differing by a multiple of three, measured from the start on the plus strand and from the end on the minus strand;
- the two genomic intervals overlap by at least 90% of the shorter one;
- the coordinate-aligned, ungapped translations over the shared codons are at least 98% identical, with `X` treated as a wildcard.

We did not require a stop-codon because many ORFs, especially in metagenomics data, run off the end of the contigs. A short CDS that lies wholly inside a longer stop-to-stop ORF can therefore match it. <!-- src: src/genome_entropy/io/genbank.py evaluate_orf_genbank_cds_match; MIN_CDS_OVERLAP_FRACTION=0.90, MIN_SHARED_AA_IDENTITY=0.98 -->

LOGAN has no deposited annotation, so its ORFs were labelled against *de novo* Prodigal 2.6.3 [@Hyatt2010-qv] gene calls (`-p meta`, contigs ≥ 120 nt) instead. <!-- src: AH genome_entropy_logan/PROJECT.md:71, :87; AH manuscript/manuscript.md:30; Table 1 -->



## Structural encoding

Translated ORFs were encoded with ModernProst (`gbouras13/modernprost-50M`) [@modernprost]. This multitask protein language model predicts two per-residue structural alphabets directly from the amino-acid sequence, with no structure or alignment as input:
- the 20-state Foldseek 3Di alphabet [@Van_Kempen2024-il];
- a 12-state alphabet.

We used model revision `2d68d506d4ce8d59025c985fd00243a8860363c6` (Table 1). Sequences were encoded in batches of up to a fixed number of residues, the *encoding size*. Because ModernProst predicts structural states from sequence alone, it assigns confident 3Di states to any protein-like sequence.

## Entropy features

For each ORF, Shannon entropy was computed in bits from the observed symbol frequencies of the sequence in a given alphabet:

$$H(X) = -\sum_{i} p_i \log_2 p_i ,$$

where $p_i$ is the fraction of positions in the ORF that carry symbol $i$. This was done separately for the nucleotide sequence (4 symbols), the amino-acid sequence (20 standard residues; the GTDB analyses also counted `X` as a symbol), the 3Di string (20) and the 12-state string (12). The maxima are therefore $\log_2 4 = 2$, $\log_2 20 \approx 4.32$, $\log_2 20$ and $\log_2 12 \approx 3.58$ bits.

The per-ORF mutual information between the aligned 3Di and 12-state strings was computed from their joint frequencies:

$$I(\mathrm{3Di};\,\mathrm{12st}) = \sum_{a,b} p(a,b)\,\log_2 \frac{p(a,b)}{p(a)\,p(b)} .$$
<!-- src: src/genome_entropy/entropy/shannon.py shannon_entropy, mutual_information -->

Two properties of these estimators shape the analyses below.
- **Length bias.** The estimators are biased downwards by an amount that shrinks with sequence length. Part of any rise of entropy with ORF length is therefore estimation bias, not biology. We quantify this against an i.i.d. reference and an empirical length-conditioned reference (Fig. S1, S2), and control for length in every comparison.
- **The log₂k ceiling.** A string that uses only $k$ symbols has entropy of at most $\log_2 k$ bits. An ORF whose predicted 3Di uses two or three states therefore has 3Di entropy of at most 1 or $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits, whatever its length.

## Statistics

The statistics shared across datasets are:
- **Discrimination:** receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) and average precision (AP).
- **Proportions:** reported with Wilson or first-order (delta-method) 95% confidence intervals.
- **Correlations:** Spearman's ρ.

Dataset-specific estimands are described under each dataset:
- the mixture estimate of real-gene share and its comparator (GTDB);
- grouped cross-validation (LOGAN, phage);
- binned mutual information with permutation nulls (phage).

Analyses used Python 3.12 with NumPy, pandas, SciPy, scikit-learn and XGBoost. The exact versions for each dataset are recorded in its analysis directory.

### Candidates and the matched comparator

Within annotated genomes, unmatched ORFs with 3Di entropy of at least 2.5 bits were split by overlap with deposited CDS coordinates on either strand:
- a **shadow** overlaps a CDS;
- a **candidate** overlaps none. <!-- src: GR §6; scripts/10_missed_genes.py overlaps_annotated() -->

The 2.5-bit threshold was set by eye, well above the 1.585-bit ceiling (Results). <!-- src: GR §10 --> ORFs running off a contig end, which have no stop codon, were flagged as contig-truncated. <!-- src: GR §6 -->

We classed shadows by frame relative to the overlapped CDS: same frame, same-strand frameshift, antisense, or frame undefined. Frame was read from the start on the plus strand and from the end on the minus strand. Frame is undefined when the CDS is partial. <!-- src: GR §6.1 "The comparator had to be fixed first" --> Same-frame shadows are largely the annotated protein, so the comparator was drawn only from antisense and frameshift shadows. That pool held 3,925,368 bacterial and 133,748 archaeal ORFs. <!-- src: MC item 1 -->

Each candidate received one partner without replacement. The partner had the same truncation status, 3Di entropy within ±0.025 bits and length within ±10%. <!-- src: MC item 2 --> Partners were sought first in the same genome, then the same 250-genome chunk, then the whole domain. Candidates left without a partner were retried with wider calipers and finally matched with replacement. In bacteria, 36.7% of partners came from the same genome and 0.16% of draws were with replacement; in archaea the figures were 26.5% and 4.3%. <!-- src: MC item 2 --> The total variation distance from the candidates on a 5 × 5 length × 3Di grid was 0.003 (bacteria) and 0.010 (archaea). <!-- src: MC "Why" table --> Comparator rates are weighted by draw multiplicity.

There were three further arms:
- annotated CDS, length-matched, as the positive control;
- `intergenic_lo`, low-3Di non-shadow ORFs, length-matched;
- the candidates' 3Di strings shuffled, as a technical null.



### Structural search and estimation

Query databases were built from amino-acid and predicted 3Di sequences. They were searched with Foldseek 10 (`10.941cd33`) [@Van_Kempen2024-il] against AlphaFold DB/Swiss-Prot (v6, 2025_03) [@Varadi2024-ze], PDB100 (2025-01-01) [@Berman2000-je], CATH50 (v4.3.0) [@Sillitoe2021-lf] and BFVD (2023_02_v2) [@Kim2025-fs]. The phage searches used a later BFVD release (Methods, *Phage genomes*). <!-- src: MC item 3; scripts/17_pilot_search.pbs; Gadi review on #137 (.version files in gtdb_entropy/foldseek_db/; PDB_DATE 250101) --> We ran a structural search (`--alignment-type 2`) and a sequence-only search (`base:search`), both at E ≤ 10⁻³, and kept the best hit by bit score. <!-- src: MC item 3; 17_pilot_search.pbs --> A hit was **full-length** when it covered at least 80% of both query and target. We did not use raw hit rate: in the top candidate 3Di quintile (≥3.22 bits in bacteria, ≥3.32 in archaea), 88–99% of every protein-like arm hits a structure database, antisense and frameshift shadows included. <!-- src: SA §2 (l.97–130); SA §12 item 5 -->

For each of Swiss-Prot, PDB100 and CATH50, the real-gene share is f = (c − s)/(a − s). Here c, s and a are the full-length hit rates of candidates, comparator and annotated CDS. <!-- src: MC "Result" --> We computed the 95% confidence interval (CI) with the delta method, treating c, s and a as independent binomial proportions with variance p(1 − p)/n. For candidates and annotated CDS, n is the number of proteins searched. For the comparator, whose rate is weighted by draw multiplicity, n is the Kish effective sample size (Σw)²/Σw², where w is the number of times each protein was drawn. Propagating these variances through f to first order gives Var(f) = [Var(c) + (1 − f)² Var(s) + f² Var(a)] / (a − s)², and the CI is f ± 1.96 √Var(f). The implied number of missed genes is f times the number of candidates, reported as the range across the three databases. BFVD was excluded from estimation, because annotated CDS reach only about 11% there. <!-- src: GR §6.1; {bac,arc}_v7_matched.tsv --> We tested three variants of the comparator weighting:
- 5 × 5 standardisation;
- unweighted;
- excluding draws made with replacement. <!-- src: GR §6.1 -->

**Direct evidence (Table 3).** These criteria need no mixture model: <!-- src: GR §6.1; scripts/22_rank_candidates.py tiers() -->
- **C1:** all candidates;
- **C2:** not truncated;
- **C3:** C2 and a full-length structural hit in any database;
- **C4:** C2 and full-length hits in at least two databases;
- **C5:** C2 and a full-length hit in Swiss-Prot, PDB100 or CATH50;
- **C6:** C2, a full-length structural hit and no full-length sequence-mode hit;
- **C7:** the strict rule, which is C4 and C5 together, plus an interpretable Swiss-Prot or PDB100 product name, at least 100 aa and best E < 10⁻¹⁰. <!-- src: GR §6.1; 22_rank_candidates.py -->

### Contigs, ORFs and Prodigal labels

Contig files were validated against S3 and seqstats (tolerance 0.1%), and contigs ≥120 nt were kept, as LOGAN does before its own gene calling. <!-- src: LMS:188-192 --> ORFs were enumerated in all six frames with genome_entropy 0.3.0 (`find_orfs`, genetic code 11). <!-- src: methods_provenance.md §2 (LOGAN row) --> We kept ORFs 30 amino acids or longer from LOGAN contigs. <!-- src: Validation/logan/README.md:139-141; results_inventory.md §5 --> ORFs running off a contig's 3′ end were trimmed to the last complete codon.

Prodigal 2.6.3 [@Hyatt2010-qv] was run on the same contigs (`prodigal -q -p meta`, GFF output); its output was byte-identical on repeated runs of all 91 pilot accessions and every 100th later accession. <!-- src: LMS:193-196; LOGAN agent review on #137 --> Prodigal supplied labels only, not the ORFs. ### Encoding and features

Translations were encoded with ModernProst (`gbouras13/modernprost-50M`, revision 2d68d506) [@modernprost] at encoding size 10,000 with genome_entropy 0.3.0 (commit 750e2b92), as recorded in all 89,994 chunk manifests, on Pawsey Setonix AMD MI250X nodes (8 GCDs × 4 encoder workers). <!-- src: #24 LOGAN agent comment ("your three requests"); methods_provenance.md §1-2; LMS:223-225 --> Amino-acid, 3Di and 12-state strings were stored one row per ORF in zstd chunks of 1 million ORFs and re-read with per-column SHA-256 checks. <!-- src: LMS:226-229 --> Per-ORF values are not bit-comparable with the V100-encoded datasets (Table S1).

Features were length (aa), the Shannon entropy (bits) of the amino-acid, 3Di and 12-state strings, and 3Di–12-state mutual information (MI), computed to match `genome_entropy.entropy.shannon` (maximum difference 2.7 × 10⁻¹⁵). <!-- src: LMS:241-247 --> Per-shard tables matched the library values on 2.7 million ORFs to within 5 × 10⁻⁵ bits, with 100% class agreement. <!-- src: LMS:251-255 -->

### Statistics and models

Single-feature separation was computed exactly from histograms over all ORFs, stratified by class, truncation, length bin, biome and tranche (0.01-bit entropy bins). <!-- src: LMS:257-260 --> We report exact ROC AUC (ties counted ½) and average precision (AP), overall and within nine length bins, and a length-stratified AUC. <!-- src: LMS:260-262 -->

Multivariable models used a class-stratified sample of 11.7 million ORFs (9.5 million complete) from all 90 shards, weighted by the inverse of each class's sampling rate. <!-- src: LMS:264-265; AH/manuscript/tables/S4_models_overall.tsv (1,760,915 + 7,751,712 complete) --> `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` (scikit-learn [@Pedregosa2011-tz]; 300 iterations, learning rate 0.1, 63 leaves, early stopping) was fitted on feature sets nested on length, with five-fold cross-validation grouped by accession, trained unweighted and evaluated population-weighted on out-of-fold scores. <!-- src: Validation/logan/scripts/13_models.py:78-85; LMS:266-268 --> The complete-ORF model was also applied unchanged to contig-end fragments.

### Six-frame experiment

A deterministic 1% hash sample of complete Prodigal+ genes gave 8.82 million genes. <!-- src: LMS:271-272; Validation/logan/scripts/14_six_frame.py:19-24 --> For each gene we took the best-overlapping Prodigal− ORF in each of the five alternative frames (same strand +1, +2; opposite strand a0–a2). ### Candidate scan and Foldseek validation

### Gene labels

An ORF was labelled a gene when it matched an annotated CDS under all of the following criteria:

- the CDS is on the same record and strand;
- the codon phase is compatible;
- the genomic overlap covers at least 90% of the shorter interval;

### Mutual information

For ORF length, the four entropies and the 3Di–12-state mutual information, we estimated binned plug-in mutual information with the label, with 20 empirical-quantile bins per axis and the 2-D product grid for pairs. <!-- src: Validation/mutual_information/README.md §3 item 4 --> Estimates were made on a class-balanced sample of 200,000 ORFs per class, where H(label) = 1 bit, and on the full population. <!-- src: Validation/mutual_information/README.md §3 item 4 -->

- **Uncertainty:** 100 bootstrap resamples, giving basic 95% intervals. <!-- src: Validation/mutual_information/README.md §3 item 4 -->
- **Null:** 200 label permutations, at 10, 20 and 40 bins. <!-- src: Validation/mutual_information/README.md §3 item 4 -->
- **Length control:** I(X; label | L), with L in 20 length-quantile strata and X re-binned within each stratum, plus single-feature ROC AUC within length bins. <!-- src: Validation/mutual_information/README.md §3 item 4 -->

## Reproducibility of the structural encoding

Re-encoding the same ORFs in the same configuration reproduced every 3Di and 12-state entropy bit for bit. Changing the encoding size changed 0.3–0.7% of per-ORF values, by at most 0.08 bits. Changing the hardware, numerical precision and package version together changed most values (only 37.8% of 3Di entropies were identical), usually by little (median 0.007 bits) but by up to 0.92 bits, enough to move an ORF across a decision threshold. Population-level quantities were unaffected (Supplementary Note 1; Table S1). We therefore report per-ORF thresholds and candidate rankings with the configuration that produced them.

# Results

## Annotated genes and spurious ORFs occupy different structural-entropy regimes

We called every six-frame ORF in 199,837 GTDB r232 species representatives: 2,568,244,984 bacterial and 54,858,398 archaeal ORFs. Because of a unit error in the package version used, every ORF is at least 90 aa long (Methods). <!-- src: Validation/gtdb/scientific_report.md l.135–141; #103 --> Of these ORFs, 12.03% (bacteria) and 17.26% (archaea) match a CDS in the genome's GenBank record: on the same strand and in the same frame, overlapping by at least 90% and with at least 98% amino-acid identity (Methods). <!-- src: GR §1, §2; src/genome_entropy/io/genbank.py:20-21 -->

In genomes that carry any annotation, matched and unmatched ORFs separate on 3Di entropy, not protein entropy (Fig. 1a–c).
- **3Di entropy:** the mean differs by 2.08 bits in bacteria (3.123 against 1.043) and by 1.99 bits in archaea (3.084 against 1.096).
- **Protein entropy:** the means differ by only 0.39 and 0.37 bits.
- **Mutual information:** 3Di–12-state MI differs threefold (0.960 against 0.321 bits in bacteria). <!-- src: GR §4.1 item 1; figG01 caption -->
- **DNA entropy:** it does not separate the classes (1.930 against 1.909 bits). <!-- src: GR §4.1 item 3 -->

## Structural entropy separates metagenomic genes from their shadows

The 50,009 LOGAN metagenomes, a random sample stratified by biome and assembly size (Methods), span 13 biomes and 4.895 Tbp of assembled contigs, and contain 89,991,549,208 six-frame ORFs of at least 30 aa (5.28 trillion amino acids). <!-- src: LMS Table 1 (l.150-161), LMS:282-288; methods_provenance.md §2; Validation/logan/scripts/01_select_accessions.py --> We classed every ORF by its relation to the genes that Prodigal called on the same contig (Fig. S3; Table 2). An ORF that matches a Prodigal gene is *Prodigal+*. Every other ORF is *Prodigal−*, and is classed by the Prodigal gene it overlaps most: an *antisense shadow* lies on the opposite strand, a *frameshift shadow* on the same strand in another frame, and an *intergenic* ORF overlaps no gene. <!-- src: Validation/logan/README.md:128-130; LMS:213-220 -->

A long ORF is one of the simplest indicators of a protein-coding gene [@Nielsen2005-rn; @Hyatt2010-qv]. ## Structural entropy identifies genes missing from GTDB annotations

We hypothesised that annotated GTDB genomes contain real protein-coding genes that their gene callers missed (Fig. S8). In the annotated genomes, 9,598,500 bacterial unmatched ORFs have 3Di entropy of at least 2.5 bits, well above the log₂(3) ceiling. Of these, 94.5% overlap an annotated CDS on either strand; these are shadows of known genes. That leaves 523,346 bacterial and 22,447 archaeal *candidates*: high-3Di ORFs that overlap no annotated CDS (Fig. 4a). <!-- src: GR §6 table; figG04_funnel.tsv.gz --> A typical annotated genome has 3 candidates in bacteria (interquartile range 1–6; maximum 5,760) and 2 in archaea (0–4). **Structural homology against a matched comparator.** A high 3Di entropy alone does not necessarily show that an ORF is a gene. Among candidates in the top quintile of 3Di entropy (≥ 3.22 bits in bacteria, ≥ 3.32 in archaea), 88–99% of every protein-like arm, antisense and frameshift shadows included, have some Foldseek hit in a structure database. <!-- src: SA §2 (l.97–130); SA §12 item 5 --> We therefore paired each candidate with a *comparator*: an antisense or frameshift shadow of an annotated CDS, almost certainly not a gene, with the same contig-truncation status, 3Di entropy within ±0.025 bits and length within ±10% (Fig. S8; Methods). ## Gene callers and structural entropy disagree in both directions in metagenomes

**Genes that Prodigal missed.** We used the gradient-boosted model trained on complete ORFs with length and four entropy features (Fig. 2; Methods) to score the 49.95 million intergenic Prodigal− ORFs of at least 100 aa in the 50,009 metagenomes. <!-- src: LMS:532-534 --> We call an ORF high-scoring when its score is at least 0.784, the score at which the model's out-of-fold precision for Prodigal genes among complete ORFs reaches 0.90 (Methods). 3,434,482 ORFs scored that highly, in 47,754 of the 50,009 metagenomes (Table S5). <!-- src: manuscript/data/tabL05_candidate_counts.tsv.gz; manuscript/data/tabL04_final_model_thresholds.tsv.gz --> The precision was calibrated on complete ORFs, so it is not the precision for these intergenic candidates. A replacement pair links an unannotated ORF that the model scores highly (p ≥ 0.8) to an annotated gene that it overlaps and that the model scores low (p ≤ 0.5, at least 0.4 below the unannotated ORF). Three limitations apply to our analyses here:

- **Short genes.** The 90-aa floor hides 28.7% of the annotated CDS; we return to short phage ORFs in a later section. <!-- src: Validation/mutual_information/README.md §2.1 (957,269 / 3,336,744) -->
- **Genetic code.** Every phage run calls ORFs with genetic code 11, including in the 104 genomes whose genes use code 4 or 15 [@Cook2024-ym].

Holding out whole ANI clusters or genera lowers AUC by only 0.0015, so near-duplicates do not inflate the metrics, but a candidate can recur in near-identical genomes and the counts are not deduplicated.

## Long ORFs with low 3Di entropy are mostly coiled coils and disordered proteins

Low 3Di entropy marks spurious ORFs, but some long annotated proteins also score low. Among annotated phage CDS, fewer than 0.1% of those between 500 and 2,000 aa score below 1 bit, but the share below 2 bits rises with length, from 3.5% at 500–800 aa to 7.7% at 800–1,200 aa and 15% above 1,200 aa. <!-- src: data/tabLL02_phage_low3di_by_length.tsv.gz --> To see what these proteins are, we examined 24 ORFs longer than 500 aa with 3Di entropy below 0.6 bits, eight each from GTDB bacteria, Inphared phages and LOGAN metagenomes (Table S15; Methods). Their DNA and amino-acid entropies are unremarkable (1.75–2.00 and 2.25–4.15 bits). Re-encoding them alone or in a batch, in fp16 or fp32, on GPU or CPU, reproduced every 3Di entropy to within 0.15 bits, so the low values are ModernProst's output and not an error of precision or of our pipeline. <!-- src: agent-handoffs projects/genome_entropy/long_low3di/data/modernprost_conditions.tsv -->

Structure predictions divide the 24 into three groups (Table S15). Eight are coiled coils or long helices, among them a SpiroCoCo coiled-coil protein, a phage tail fibre and a Rad50-like protein. ESMFold2 models seven of them confidently (mean pLDDT 78–85), and Foldseek's 3Di of these models is itself low, 0.7–1.8 bits and mostly the helix state V; the Rad50-like protein scores 2.0 bits in its AlphaFold 3 model. ModernProst sharpens this further, to 0.17–0.54 bits. <!-- src: data/tabLL01_long_low3di.tsv.gz -->

Thirteen are disordered or poorly predicted. AlphaFold 3, run on the full ORF with an MSA, predicts nine to be almost wholly disordered (fraction disordered 0.90–1.00; mean pLDDT 35–49). The 3Di of these models is dominated by D (0.20–1.20 bits), as ModernProst's is (0.00–0.50 bits), and the two strings agree at 72–97% of residues. These proteins are not simply orphans. Their MSAs hold 0 to 2,755 sequences (Neff 1–104), and the three with the deepest alignments (Neff 83–104), resembling a TET dioxygenase, the yeast laminarase-resistance protein LRE1 and, in part, an *Arabidopsis* protein, are as disordered as those with no homologues. Four more, from phages and an uncultured bacterium, get compact but unconfident models from both predictors (AlphaFold 3 pLDDT 33–40, fraction disordered 0.19–0.49; Neff 2–41), so their structure remains unresolved. <!-- src: data/tabLL01_long_low3di.tsv.gz; agent-handoffs projects/genome_entropy/long_low3di/data/af3_vs_modernprost.tsv -->

The remaining three are failures of the encoder. The major head protein of a microvirus (535 aa) folds confidently (ESMFold2 mean pLDDT 83, with 80% of residues above 70), and Foldseek gives its model 3.71 bits, within the 3.4–4.0 bits of the 12 control phage proteins, yet ModernProst gives 0.57 bits, with 94% D or V in the ordered regions. Encoded in 256-aa tiles it scores 2.0 bits, and its entropy falls as more of the sequence is encoded, so the collapse depends on the length of the context. <!-- src: agent-handoffs projects/genome_entropy/long_low3di/data/structure_vs_modernprost.tsv, modernprost_conditions.tsv --> Two very long proteins (3,488 and 7,962 aa) show the same behaviour in part: over their ordered regions, Foldseek gives 3.23 and 2.13 bits and ModernProst 1.76 and 1.33. <!-- src: work/long_low3di/structure_vs_modernprost.tsv (E06, E04; mp/struct_entropy_ordered) -->

Low 3Di entropy in a long ORF therefore usually reflects low structural complexity, a coiled coil or a disordered protein, rather than a spurious ORF. ModernProst assigns such proteins a single state more uniformly than structure-derived 3Di does, and occasionally does the same to a folded protein, so low 3Di entropy alone should not be read as evidence that a long ORF is non-coding.

## Short ORFs mark the method's lower length limit

The Global Microbial smORF Catalogue (GMSC) holds 965 million non-redundant small ORFs (smORFs, 10–99 aa) from 63,410 metagenomes and 87,920 isolate genomes [@Duan2024-ab]. Its authors provide a curated benchmark, with the 33.6 million positive ORFs deemed as high-quality smORFs from families with coding potential predicted by RNAcode [@Washietl2011-yi] and support from metatranscriptomes, ribosome profiling or metaproteomes, or with close homologues among known small proteins. The 10.1 million negatives are conserved, in-frame GMSC sequences from clusters that RNAcode called non-coding, with no domain hits and no homology to the positives (Methods). <!-- src: Duan et al. 2024 Methods (PMC11364881); AH smorfs/manuscript.md §2.1; Validation/smorfs/README.md:4, :23 --> The negatives therefore look protein-like by construction, which makes this a hard benchmark.

On this benchmark, structural entropy separates real from spurious small ORFs only weakly (Fig. 7). Amino-acid entropy shows the same thing: on phage ORFs it separates the classes as well as 3Di entropy from 50 aa, and better below 50 aa, whereas on GMSC smORFs it is near or below chance. The phage result therefore shows how much the separation depends on the negative set: the weak separation of GMSC smORFs mostly reflects how that benchmark's negatives were chosen.

These results set the method's lower length limits. Phage ORFs of at least 100 aa separate well: 3Di entropy alone reaches AUC 0.947. <!-- src: Validation/mutual_information/README.md §4.6 --> From 50 to 99 aa it still does a reasonable job on phages (AUC 0.71–0.85), although less well than above 100 aa, and below 50 aa it carries little signal even against phage shadows. There are therefore two thresholds: above about 100 aa structural entropy works well, below about 50 aa it is not useful, and between them it is intermediate. Against conserved, coding-like negatives such as those of GMSC, a single entropy value for each alphabet carries only a small signal below 100 aa. Much of this weakness is probably length itself. A 3Di string of 50–99 symbols gives few observations for a 20-state distribution, and many real small proteins may be a single helix or disordered. Entropy ignores the order of the states: a string of alternating helix and strand states and the same states sorted into two blocks have the same entropy.

# Discussion

Gene callers decide which open reading frames are genes from nucleotide statistics: codon usage, start codons, ribosome-binding sites and the length of the open frame [@Hyatt2010-qv; @Besemer2001-un; @Delcher2007-uf]. These callers are accurate on complete bacterial genomes, but the errors that remain have been recognised for two decades. Comparisons of annotations with independent predictions found genomes with too many short genes annotated and others with genes missing [@Nielsen2005-rn], tens of thousands of missing genes across prokaryotic genomes [@Warren2010-mg], and inconsistent starts, missed genes and split genes that curation pipelines such as GenePRIMP flag for review [@Pati2010-qy]. Small genes in particular are often overlooked [@Sberro2019-sm; @Duan2024-ab], and phage gene callers such as PHANOTATE were developed partly because phage genes are short and overlapping [@McNair2019-yt]. Each of these checks compares one gene caller with another, or with homology to known proteins. Here we used a different signal: whether the translated ORF looks like a folded protein on the basis of its statistical profile.

Across bacterial and archaeal genomes, metagenomes and phages, the entropy of the predicted 3Di structural string separated annotated genes from other ORFs far better than amino-acid composition. In GTDB genomes the 3Di gap between matched and unmatched ORFs was five times the amino-acid gap. In 50,009 metagenomes 3Di entropy separated genes from non-genes within fixed length bins, where length alone did not. In phages it carried 0.587 bits of information about annotation, and amino-acid entropy added only 0.057 bits. Earlier work, including our own, used the composition and entropy of amino-acid and nucleotide sequences to describe genomes and to identify coding ORFs [@Akhter2013-pl; @McNair2021-zy]. Our results suggest that, once predicted structure is available, composition adds little. This agrees with recent work treating the 3Di alphabet as an information-bearing view of a protein family that complements its amino-acid sequence [@Shukla2026-mk]. That work finds that the reduced 3Di alphabet lowers the effective entropy of a family alignment and is most useful when alignments are shallow or divergent [@Shukla2026-mk], conditions typical of the unannotated ORFs we score.

**Why structural entropy separates genes from spurious ORFs.** Spurious translations rarely resemble folded proteins. AntiFam collects families of such translations [@Eberhardt2012-af], and AlphaFold predicts no globular structure for almost all of them [@Monzon2022-fu]. Predicted 3Di strings show the same thing at the level of single ORFs. The 3Di entropy of unannotated ORFs fall below log₂3, the most that a string of three 3Di states can reach, and three states, D, V and P, made up 99.4% of their residues. These are the states that dominate such 3Di strings, and D and P mark residues outside helices and strands [@Heinzinger2024-ja]. A low 3Di entropy therefore means that the language model (which itself is trained on AlphaFold2 structure predictions) likely predicts little regular structure along the ORF, suggesting a low quality structure prediction. The signal also belongs to the translated frame, not to the DNA. Length and amino-acid entropy are correlated between a gene and the ORFs in its other five frames, because those ORFs share its nucleotide composition and stop-codon spacing. 3Di entropy is not correlated between frames, and the gene's own frame has the highest 3Di entropy of the six in 92.4% of genes. The score therefore follows the protein that a frame would encode, which is what lets it choose between overlapping ORFs.

**Gene callers and structural entropy disagree in both directions.** In GTDB genomes we estimate that 51,000–59,000 unannotated ORFs are protein-coding genes, against a comparator matched on length, 3Di entropy and truncation. That is about one missed gene for every two annotated genomes. A strict individual test is passed by 25,114 candidates, and several examples we analyses are biologically coherent: a Cas5 that completes a type I CRISPR–*cas* operon, and a SusC that completes a polysaccharide utilisation locus, showing this approach can be used to refine and improve genome annotations. In metagenomes, high-scoring intergenic ORFs that Prodigal skipped had full-length structural homologues 90–200 times as often as matched controls. For those with bacterial homologues, Prodigal's own scores fell just short of its decision boundary, mostly because of weak coding scores and missing ribosome-binding sites under the model chosen for the contig. These look like genes from organisms whose codon usage and start signals the metagenomic models do not represent; structurally supported ORFs could serve as additional training examples for those models. In the other direction, 17.4% of complete Prodigal genes in metagenomes score low, and outside the shortest lengths these rarely have structural homologues, consistent with the over-annotation of short genes reported before [@Nielsen2005-rn].

A structural score therefore complements a gene caller rather than replacing it, and it is most useful where the two disagree. It does not show that an ORF is expressed or functional, so the disagreements are best treated as a ranked list for transcriptomic, proteomic or experimental confirmation.

**Refining automated phage annotation.** Phage annotation pipelines such as Pharokka and Phold annotate the function of the genes that a gene caller reports [@Bouras2023-wx; @Bouras2026-yi; @Bouras2026-vu], so the gene models set the limit of what they can describe. The choice of gene caller and of genetic code already changes those models [@McNair2019-yt; @Cook2024-ym]. For instance, in OM869708, the Pyrodigal-gv calls used for re-annotation dropped a replication initiator gene that the genome's submitters had annotated, and called a hypothetical protein in another frame instead. The structural score preferred the submitters' gene, and independent homologues support it. The score is cheap to compute. A typical 5-Mb bacterial genome has about 18,000 ORFs of at least 90 aa (3.5 million amino acids), which ModernProst encodes in about 16 s on one AMD MI250X GPU at the throughput of our metagenome run, and in under a minute on an NVIDIA V100. <!-- src: work/review_facts/answers.md §12 (659-genome GTDB sample: ~692,000 aa per Mb, mean ORF 216 aa; LOGAN 215–220 k aa/s per GCD; V100 ~75 k aa/s estimated from the GMSC run, approximate). ORF calling and entropy on CPU not timed. --> Structural entropy could therefore be run alongside an annotation pipeline to flag loci where the chosen gene model looks unlike a protein, and the reverse.

**The primary limitation is short ORFs.** Structural entropy struggles on short proteins. Below about 100 amino acids the signal weakens: on curated small ORFs all features together reach AUC 0.73, and below 30 amino acids 3Di entropy is uninformative. Short sequences are a known difficulty for structure prediction, whose confidence scores are least reliable for short spurious proteins [@Monzon2022-fu], and a 3Di string of a few dozen symbols gives few observations for a 20-state distribution. On phage ORFs, 3Di entropy separates genes only weakly below 50 amino acids (AUC 0.57), and from 50 amino acids it does better largely because unannotated phage ORFs are less protein-like than the GMSC negatives. Several of our datasets have an effective 90-amino-acid floor. However, the phage genomes, re-run down to 10 amino acids, show that 90-100 amino acids is an approximate threshold where the 3di entropy method starts to reduce in efficacy.

Another limitation of our analysis is that the labels are annotations or Prodigal calls, not ground truth, so every comparison measures agreement with a gene caller. Finally, we caution against applying structural entropy to ORF calls from eukaryotic sequence in metagenomes. Part of what Prodigal misses there is probably eukaryotic coding sequence, which neither Prodigal, designed for prokaryotes, nor our controls were built for. A better and more eukaryote-appropriate gene caller would need to be used, with 3Di entropy analyses would need to be reconducted on such a dataset to be confident of its performance on eukaryotic genomes.

# Supplementary note 1: reproducibility of the structural encoding

The datasets in this paper were encoded with different package versions, encoding sizes and hardware (Table 1). To separate run-to-run variation from the effects of configuration, we re-encoded the first 50 genomes of the Gadi phage encoding (6,284 ORFs). <!-- src: AH inphared_gadi/README.md §5; data/tabG01_encoding_determinism.tsv.gz (n_orfs) --> We used genome_entropy 0.3.0 and `gbouras13/modernprost-50M` on one NVIDIA V100, at encoding sizes of 10,000 and 20,000 residues. <!-- src: AH inphared_gadi/README.md §5; inphared_gadi/scripts/50_determinism.pbs (ngpus=1) -->

We compared these re-encodings with two earlier encodings (Table S1):

- the production run they repeat (V100, encoding size 10,000);
- an independent encoding of the same ORFs on AMD GPUs on Pawsey Setonix (ROCm, fp16, genome_entropy 0.2.0, encoding size 20,000). <!-- src: data/tabG01_encoding_determinism.tsv.gz (run_a, run_b) -->

The comparisons gave three results.

- **Repeat run.** Repeating the production configuration reproduced every 3Di entropy, 12-state entropy and 3Di–12-state mutual information value bit for bit. <!-- src: data/tabG01_encoding_determinism.tsv.gz rows "det es10k vs production" (100%) -->
- **Encoding size.** Changing the encoding size changed 0.3–0.7% of ORFs, by at most 0.076 bits. <!-- src: data/tabG01_encoding_determinism.tsv.gz rows "det es20k vs production" (99.68/99.60/99.35% identical; max 0.0758) -->
- **Hardware, precision and package version.** Between the two systems only 37.8% of 3Di entropies were bit-identical. <!-- src: data/tabG01_encoding_determinism.tsv.gz row "det es10k vs Setonix", three_di_entropy --> The median absolute difference was 0.007 bits, the 99th percentile 0.31 bits and the maximum 0.92 bits (Pearson r = 0.997). <!-- src: data/tabG01_encoding_determinism.tsv.gz (0.0066, 0.310, 0.920, 0.99719) --> Matching the encoding size left this spread unchanged. <!-- src: data/tabG01_encoding_determinism.tsv.gz row "det es20k vs Setonix" (37.7%, 0.310, 0.920) -->

We could not re-run the Setonix side in the identical configuration. The design therefore cannot apportion the residual between hardware, numerical precision and package version. The 50 genomes reproduce the whole-population agreement between the two systems (99th percentile 0.310 against 0.317 bits; r = 0.99719 against 0.99698). <!-- src: AH inphared_gadi/README.md §5 point 3 -->

Population-level quantities are unaffected: class means move by about 0.01 bits, against a 1.46-bit difference between classes. <!-- src: AH inphared_gadi/README.md §5 ("Consequences") --> Per-ORF entropies are not reproducible bit for bit across platforms.
